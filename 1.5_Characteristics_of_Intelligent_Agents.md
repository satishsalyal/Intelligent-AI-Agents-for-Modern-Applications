## 1.5.1 Introduction

> Intelligent agents are an important component of modern Artificial Intelligence (AI). They are designed to perceive their environment, process information, make decisions, and perform actions to achieve specific objectives. Their intelligence is reflected not merely in their ability to respond to inputs, but also in their ability to select appropriate actions, adapt to changing conditions, and evaluate the outcomes of their behaviour.

The characteristics of an intelligent agent determine how effectively it operates in a particular environment. For example, a simple rule-based agent may respond to a predefined condition, whereas a learning-based agent may improve its decisions using experience. Similarly, a conversational AI system may answer a question, while an autonomous task-oriented agent may plan several steps, invoke external tools, and monitor progress towards a goal.

Understanding these characteristics is essential for designing intelligent systems for healthcare, education, finance, robotics, cybersecurity, scientific research, and business automation.

The principal characteristics of intelligent agents include:

- [x] **Autonomy**
- [x] **Reactivity**
- [x] **Proactiveness**
- [x] **Goal-oriented behaviour**
- [x] **Rationality**
- [x] **Learning and adaptability**
- [x] **Social ability**
- [x] **Perception and environmental awareness**
- [x] **Memory and state management**
- [x] **Persistence and task execution**
- [x] **Robustness and fault tolerance**

> [!NOTE]
> Not every intelligent agent possesses all these characteristics to the same degree. Their implementation depends on the agent's architecture, available information, computational resources, objectives, and operating environment.

## 1.5.2 Autonomy

Autonomy refers to an agent's ability to operate and make decisions without requiring continuous direct instructions from a human user.

An autonomous agent can select actions based on its objectives, observations, internal state, and available knowledge. The degree of autonomy varies considerably among different types of agents.

For example, a traditional thermostat automatically switches a heating system on or off according to a temperature threshold. A more sophisticated building-management agent may analyse temperature, occupancy, weather forecasts, and energy prices before adjusting heating and cooling schedules.

### Characteristics of autonomy

| # | Characteristic |
|---|---|
| 1 | Makes decisions using available information and predefined objectives. |
| 2 | Performs appropriate actions without requiring approval for every step. |
| 3 | Maintains operational continuity within its authorised boundaries. |
| 4 | Can handle routine situations independently. |
| 5 | Requests human assistance when a decision exceeds its permissions or capabilities. |

### Example: University information assistant

An autonomous university information agent might retrieve the latest examination timetable from an authorised university source, identify the relevant examination date, and prepare a response for a student. It does not need a human operator to guide every retrieval step.

However, publishing an official timetable or changing a student's academic record may require explicit authorisation.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Important point:** Autonomy does not mean unrestricted authority. A responsible agent operates within defined permissions, security policies, and human-oversight requirements.

## 1.5.3 Reactivity

Reactivity is the ability of an intelligent agent to perceive changes in its environment and respond appropriately.

Real-world environments are rarely static. Information changes, resources become unavailable, user requirements evolve, and unexpected events occur. An agent must therefore detect relevant changes and adjust its behaviour when necessary.

A reactive agent follows a general cycle:

| Step | Description |
|---|---|
| **Perceive the environment** | Collect observations, events, or sensor data |
| **Interpret the change** | Determine whether the current situation requires a response |
| **Select and perform an action** | Respond according to rules, goals, or a decision policy |
| **Observe the result** | Check the outcome and continue monitoring |

### Example: Network monitoring agent

A network-monitoring agent continuously observes traffic and system performance. If it detects unusual traffic, excessive latency, or repeated failed login attempts, it may raise an alert or initiate a permitted response.

Its reactive behaviour allows it to respond to changing conditions rather than relying only on a previously prepared plan.

### Reactivity versus autonomy

These characteristics are related but different:

- **Autonomy** concerns how independently an agent operates.
- **Reactivity** concerns how effectively it responds to changes.

An agent may operate autonomously but respond poorly to unexpected changes. Conversely, a system may react quickly to events while requiring human approval before taking action.

## 1.5.4 Proactiveness

Proactiveness is the ability of an agent to initiate actions in anticipation of future needs or to make progress towards an objective without waiting for a direct instruction at every step.

A purely reactive system responds when a particular event occurs. A proactive system may anticipate events, identify opportunities, or initiate suitable actions before a problem becomes urgent.

For example, a reactive server-monitoring system might issue an alert after a server exceeds its memory threshold. A proactive system may analyse resource trends and forecast a potential capacity problem before the threshold is reached.

### Example: Intelligent academic-support agent

Consider an agent designed to help students prepare for examinations. With authorised access to examination dates and a student's study preferences, it could:

1. Identify upcoming examinations.
2. Estimate the time available for preparation.
3. Detect topics that have not yet been covered.
4. Propose a revision schedule.
5. Remind the student about planned activities, if reminders are enabled.

The agent takes initiative by identifying and recommending useful actions rather than merely answering questions.

> [!TIP]
> **Distinguishing feature:** Proactiveness involves initiative towards an objective. It does not imply that an agent should act without limits or disregard user preferences.

## 1.5.5 Goal-Oriented Behaviour

Goal-oriented behaviour refers to an agent's ability to select actions according to a desired outcome.

A goal defines what the agent is attempting to accomplish. Actions are evaluated according to their contribution towards achieving that goal.

For example, an AI agent tasked with organising a research literature review might need to identify relevant papers, extract research questions, group studies by methodology, and prepare a structured summary. These actions serve a common objective.

A goal-oriented agent generally performs the following activities:

1. Interpret the objective.
2. Identify the current state and the desired state.
3. Determine the tasks required to bridge the difference.
4. Select an appropriate sequence of actions.
5. Monitor progress.
6. Revise the plan when necessary.
7. Determine whether the goal has been achieved.

The distinction between goals and actions is important. Searching a database is an action; locating a specified set of relevant papers is a goal.

### Goal representation

A simplified representation is:

```
G = Desired outcome

π* = A suitable policy or plan for pursuing G
```

Here, $\pi^*$ represents a selected policy or plan, not necessarily a mathematically optimal solution. The agent chooses actions based on the available information, constraints, and decision procedure.

An agent may fail to achieve its goal because of incomplete information, unavailable resources, or environmental changes. Goal-oriented behaviour therefore requires progress monitoring rather than merely executing a fixed list of steps.

## 1.5.6 Rationality

Rationality is the ability of an agent to select actions expected to produce the best outcome according to a defined performance measure, given its available information and computational resources.

A rational agent does not necessarily possess complete knowledge or always make the objectively best decision. Instead, it chooses an action that is reasonable in light of its observations, beliefs, objectives, and constraints.

For example, a delivery-planning agent may compare several routes using estimated travel time, distance, traffic, and delivery deadlines. It selects a route according to its performance criteria rather than choosing arbitrarily.

### Factors affecting rational decisions

- Available observations and their accuracy.
- Knowledge of the environment.
- Defined goals and performance measures.
- Possible actions and their consequences.
- Resource constraints, including time and computation.
- Uncertainty about future events.

### Rationality and optimality

Rationality should not be confused with guaranteed optimality. An agent may make a rational choice using incomplete information and still obtain an unsatisfactory result.

For example, a route-planning agent may choose the fastest known route based on current traffic data, but an unexpected road closure may make that route slower than an alternative.

Rational behaviour depends on the information and decision criteria available when the action is selected.

## 1.5.7 Learning and Adaptability

Learning is the ability of an agent to improve its behaviour using data, experience, feedback, or changes in its knowledge.

Adaptability is the ability to modify behaviour when the environment, requirements, or operating conditions change.

Although closely related, these concepts are not identical. An agent can adapt by applying predefined rules without learning a new model. A learning agent uses experience or data to modify some aspect of its future behaviour.

### Common learning mechanisms

| Mechanism | Description |
|---|---|
| **Supervised learning** | Learns patterns from labelled examples. |
| **Unsupervised learning** | Identifies patterns or structure in unlabelled data. |
| **Reinforcement learning** | Learns a policy through interactions and reward signals. |
| **Feedback-based refinement** | Uses observed outcomes or evaluations to improve subsequent actions. |
| **Knowledge updating** | Incorporates new, verified information into its accessible knowledge sources. |

### Example: Personalised learning agent

An educational agent may observe that a student frequently makes mistakes when solving Python loop problems. With appropriate permissions and a reliable evaluation mechanism, it can recommend additional exercises, provide simpler explanations, and adjust the difficulty of subsequent tasks.

The objective is not merely to repeat the same response, but to tailor future assistance based on relevant evidence.

> [!CAUTION]
> However, an LLM-based agent does not automatically learn permanently from every conversation. Learning may require explicit model training, a feedback mechanism, stored preferences, an updated knowledge base, or another deliberate design feature.

## 1.5.8 Social Ability and Communication

Social ability is the capacity of an agent to communicate and coordinate with humans, other agents, or external systems.

Intelligent agents frequently operate as components of larger systems. Effective communication enables them to exchange information, delegate tasks, request assistance, and coordinate activities.

### Forms of communication

- Natural-language interaction with human users.
- Structured data exchange through APIs.
- Message passing between software agents.
- Coordination through shared task states or databases.
- Requests for clarification or human approval.

### Example: Multi-agent research assistant

A multi-agent research system may assign different responsibilities to specialised agents:

- A search agent identifies potentially relevant papers.
- An extraction agent collects bibliographic information and research findings.
- An analysis agent groups studies by methods and objectives.
- A writing agent prepares a structured draft.
- A review agent checks whether claims are supported by the collected sources.

These components must exchange information and coordinate their work. Their effectiveness depends not only on individual capabilities but also on the quality of communication, task allocation, and result verification.

> [!WARNING]
> Social ability is particularly important in multi-agent systems, where poorly coordinated agents may duplicate work, exchange inconsistent information, or reach conflicting conclusions.

## 1.5.9 Perception and Environmental Awareness

An intelligent agent needs information about its environment to make informed decisions. Perception refers to the process of collecting and interpreting observations, while environmental awareness concerns the agent's representation of the relevant situation.

The source of observations depends on the application.

| Environment | Possible observations |
|---|---|
| Robotics | Camera images, distance sensors, position, and motion |
| Healthcare support | Authorised clinical records, measurements, and user input |
| Cybersecurity | Logs, network events, access attempts, and alerts |
| Education | Syllabus documents, examination dates, and student-submitted goals |
| Business automation | Database records, transaction states, and API responses |
| Web-based research | Search results, documents, metadata, and source content |

An agent may receive observations directly through sensors, indirectly through software tools, or through a human user.

Perception does not guarantee that the observations are correct or complete. A camera may be obscured, a database may be outdated, or a document may contain incorrect information. Consequently, reliable agents should account for uncertainty and verify important observations when possible.

## 1.5.10 Memory and State Management

Memory enables an agent to retain information that may be useful for subsequent decisions. State management allows the system to track the current status of a task, including completed actions, pending operations, constraints, and previous observations.

Memory can be organised into several forms.

### 1. Working or conversational memory

Maintains information relevant to the current interaction or task, such as the user's latest instructions and the steps already completed.

### 2. Long-term or persistent memory

Stores selected information across sessions when the system is explicitly designed and authorised to do so, such as user preferences or previously established project details.

### 3. External knowledge

Uses searchable documents, databases, or vector stores to retrieve information when needed, rather than keeping all information directly in the model's active context.

For example, a research agent working on a systematic review may record which papers have been screened, which studies require full-text inspection, and which references have already been extracted. Without appropriate state management, it may repeat completed work or omit important steps.

> [!NOTE]
> Memory must also be managed responsibly. Systems should avoid retaining unnecessary sensitive information, protect stored data, and distinguish verified facts from uncertain or outdated information.

## 1.5.11 Persistence and Task Execution

Persistence refers to an agent's ability to continue pursuing a task across multiple steps rather than stopping after a single response. Task execution involves carrying out the actions needed to produce a result.

A persistent agent may need to:

- Track task progress.
- Maintain intermediate results.
- Retry recoverable operations.
- Resume an interrupted workflow.
- Detect when a task is complete.
- Stop when further attempts are unproductive or unsafe.

Consider an agent that generates a report from several approved documents. If one document cannot be retrieved, the agent may retry the operation, continue processing the available sources, and report the missing document rather than silently treating the task as complete.

> [!TIP]
> Persistence does not mean repeating actions indefinitely. A well-designed agent should have limits on retries, time, and resource use, and should know when to request human assistance.

## 1.5.12 Robustness and Fault Tolerance

Robustness is the ability of an agent to continue operating appropriately despite unexpected inputs, changing conditions, or imperfect information.

Fault tolerance is the ability to handle certain failures without causing the entire system to fail.

These characteristics are especially important for agents that interact with external services, APIs, databases, and physical devices.

For example, a travel-planning agent may encounter an unavailable booking service. Rather than inventing a confirmation, it should identify the failed operation, try an authorised alternative if appropriate, and communicate the limitation.

### Common strategies include:

- Input validation.
- Error detection and handling.
- Bounded retries.
- Timeouts and fallback procedures.
- Verification of tool outputs.
- Logging and monitoring.
- Human escalation for unresolved failures.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Robustness is not the ability to avoid every error. It is the ability to detect, manage, and recover from errors in a controlled manner.

## 1.5.13 Summary of the Characteristics of Intelligent Agents

The following table consolidates the main characteristics discussed in this section.

| Characteristic | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Autonomy** | Operates without continuous direct control | Automatically retrieves approved timetable information |
| **Reactivity** | Responds to environmental changes | Detects a network anomaly and raises an alert |
| **Proactiveness** | Initiates actions towards objectives | Recommends revision activities before an examination |
| **Goal-oriented behaviour** | Selects actions to achieve a desired outcome | Creates a structured study plan |
| **Rationality** | Selects actions according to expected performance | Chooses a route using travel-time estimates |
| **Learning** | Improves behaviour using data or experience | Refines recommendations from evaluated outcomes |
| **Adaptability** | Adjusts to changing conditions | Changes a plan when a deadline changes |
| **Social ability** | Communicates and coordinates | Exchanges results with specialised agents |
| **Perception** | Collects and interprets observations | Reads authorised documents or sensor data |
| **Memory** | Retains relevant information | Tracks completed research tasks |
| **Persistence** | Continues a task across multiple steps | Resumes an interrupted document-processing workflow |
| **Robustness** | Handles unexpected conditions and failures | Reports an unavailable service and follows a fallback procedure |

## 1.5.14 Applying the Characteristics to a Single Intelligent Agent

Consider an intelligent university academic-support agent whose objective is to help a student prepare for examinations using authorised academic information.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> **Objective**
>
> Prepare a feasible, personalised examination study plan

### Agent characteristics in operation

| Characteristic | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| **Perception** | Retrieves authorised syllabus and examination information. |
| **Rationality and goal orientation** | Prioritises topics according to deadlines and the student's stated needs. |
| **Proactiveness** | Suggests a schedule before the examination date. |
| **Memory and adaptability** | Tracks completed topics and revises the plan when requirements change. |
| **Robustness and autonomy** | Handles unavailable sources safely and operates only within authorised permissions. |

> [!NOTE]
> **Expected outcome**
>
> A checked study plan presented to the student, with confirmation required before any consequential external action

This example illustrates that intelligent behaviour usually emerges from the combination of multiple characteristics rather than a single capability. Perception supplies information, memory preserves task state, goal orientation guides planning, rationality supports action selection, and feedback enables the agent to revise its approach.

---

## 1.5.15 Conclusion

The characteristics of intelligent agents provide a foundation for understanding how AI systems move from simple input-response behaviour towards more capable, goal-directed operation.

Autonomy enables independent operation within defined boundaries, reactivity supports timely responses, and proactiveness enables initiative. Goal orientation and rationality guide action selection, while learning and adaptability support improvement under changing conditions. Social ability, perception, memory, persistence, and robustness help agents operate effectively in complex environments.

These characteristics should not be interpreted as an all-or-nothing checklist. Different applications require different combinations and levels of capability. A rule-based monitoring agent may be highly reactive but have limited learning ability, whereas an LLM-based research agent may use planning, memory, and tools without necessarily possessing unrestricted autonomy or reliable self-improvement.

The design of an effective intelligent agent therefore requires a careful balance among capability, reliability, safety, efficiency, and human oversight.
